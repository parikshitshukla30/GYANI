Metaphysical Concepts in Indian Thought

Atman, Brahman, Maya, Karma, the nature of reality and Moksha in Indian philosophical thought.

01

Introduction to Metaphysical Concepts in Indian Thought

Metaphysics is the branch of philosophy concerned with fundamental questions about reality, existence, the self, consciousness, causation and the ultimate nature of things. Indian philosophical traditions developed extensive discussions on these questions, although different schools reached different conclusions.

Indian philosophical inquiry does not consist of one single philosophical system. Vedanta, Samkhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa, Buddhism, Jainism and Charvaka developed distinct positions concerning the self, world, knowledge, action and liberation.

Major Questions of Metaphysical Inquiry

  • What is ultimately real?
  • What is the nature of the self?
  • What is the relationship between the individual, the world and ultimate reality?
  • What is the relationship between action and its consequences?
  • Is liberation possible, and if so, what is the nature of liberation?
Key Point

Atman, Brahman, Maya, Karma and Moksha are important concepts in major Indian philosophical discussions, but their meanings and philosophical status differ from one school to another.

02

Atman: The Concept of Self

Atman is a major concept in Upanishadic and Vedantic thought and is generally associated with the self or the innermost principle of an individual. The concept concerns the question of what a person fundamentally is beyond temporary physical characteristics.

In several Upanishadic and Vedantic interpretations, Atman is distinguished from the body, senses and changing mental states. In Advaita Vedanta, Atman is ultimately identified with Brahman. However, this identification should not be generalized to every Indian philosophical school.

Atman in Upanishadic Thought

The Upanishads contain important discussions concerning the nature of the self and its relationship with ultimate reality. A famous teaching in the Chandogya Upanishad is expressed through the statement 'Tat Tvam Asi', traditionally translated as 'That Thou Art'. In its philosophical context, the statement is associated with the identity of the individual self with the ultimate principle.

Different Views of the Self

Vedanta

Different Vedanta schools explain the relationship between Atman, Brahman and the individual in different ways. Advaita Vedanta teaches ultimate non-duality, while other Vedanta schools maintain distinctions between the individual self and Brahman.

Samkhya

Samkhya distinguishes conscious Purusha from unconscious Prakriti. The individual experiences bondage because of failure to discriminate between them.

Buddhism

Buddhist philosophy rejects the idea of a permanent, unchanging self through the doctrine of Anatta or Anatman.

Jainism

Jain philosophy accepts Jiva as an enduring conscious substance. Karma is understood as a form of bondage that obscures the soul's inherent qualities.

Important Distinction

It is incorrect to say that all Indian philosophical schools teach the same concept of Atman. Vedanta, Samkhya, Jainism and Buddhism have significantly different positions concerning the self.

03

Brahman: The Concept of Ultimate Reality

Brahman is a central concept in the Upanishadic and Vedanta traditions. In Vedantic philosophy, it refers to ultimate reality, although different Vedanta schools interpret its nature and relationship with the world and individual self differently.

Brahman in the Upanishads

The Upanishads investigate Brahman as the fundamental reality underlying existence. Some Upanishadic passages describe Brahman through expressions concerning being, consciousness and bliss, while also emphasizing that ultimate reality cannot be completely captured by ordinary concepts and descriptions.

Brahman in Major Vedanta Schools

Advaita Vedanta

Advaita Vedanta, associated especially with Shankaracharya, teaches that Brahman is the ultimate non-dual reality. The apparent plurality of the world is explained through Maya and ignorance.

Vishishtadvaita

Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita accepts Brahman as the supreme reality while understanding individual souls and matter as real and related to Brahman.

Dvaita

Madhva's Dvaita maintains a real distinction between God, individual souls and matter. Brahman is identified with the supreme personal God, Vishnu.

Key Point

Brahman is primarily a concept of the Upanishadic and Vedanta traditions. It should not be presented as a concept interpreted identically by every Indian philosophical school.

04

Maya: Appearance, Ignorance and Reality

Maya is an important concept particularly in Advaita Vedanta. It is used to explain why the world is experienced as a plurality of separate objects and beings even though ultimate reality is understood as non-dual Brahman.

Maya should not be reduced to the simple statement that 'the world does not exist'. Advaita makes more precise distinctions concerning different levels or statuses of reality and the role of ignorance in ordinary experience.

Maya and Avidya

Avidya means ignorance or lack of true knowledge. In Advaita Vedanta, ignorance contributes to the mistaken identification of the self with the body, mind and limited individuality. Knowledge of the true nature of the self removes this ignorance.

Important Characteristics

  • Maya is especially important in Advaita Vedanta.
  • It is connected with the appearance of multiplicity.
  • It is discussed together with ignorance and knowledge.
  • It is not accurately explained simply as ordinary illusion or non-existence.
Important Distinction

Maya is not a universal doctrine shared in exactly the same form by all Indian philosophical schools. Its systematic metaphysical role is particularly associated with Advaita Vedanta.

05

Ontological Implications of Atman, Brahman and Maya

Ontology is the philosophical study of being, existence and what is real. Questions concerning Atman, Brahman and Maya therefore become questions about the status of the self, the world and ultimate reality.

Atman

Raises the fundamental question: What is the true nature of the self? Is the self merely the body and mind, or is there a deeper principle of identity?

Brahman

Raises the question of whether there is an ultimate, fundamental reality underlying the changing world of experience.

Maya

Raises questions about why reality appears in the form of multiplicity and how ordinary perception relates to ultimate reality.

Different Schools

Different schools provide different answers. Therefore, Indian philosophy should be studied comparatively rather than as a single uniform doctrine.

Core Idea

The concepts of Atman, Brahman and Maya become especially significant when studying the relationship between individual identity, experienced reality and ultimate reality in Vedantic philosophy.

06

Karma and Its Role in Indian Philosophy

The Sanskrit word karma literally refers to action or deed. In Indian philosophical and religious traditions, karma developed into a broader doctrine concerning actions and their consequences.

The doctrine of karma is closely related to ethical responsibility. Actions are not treated as completely disconnected events; they can have consequences for the individual according to the philosophical framework of the particular tradition.

Karma and Ethical Responsibility

The concept of karma provides a connection between conduct and consequences. It therefore became an important part of discussions about moral responsibility, duty, discipline and the consequences of actions.

Karma and Rebirth

Many Indian traditions connect karma with Sansar, the cycle of repeated birth and death. However, the exact explanation of karma, rebirth and the entity that undergoes rebirth differs among traditions.

Three Common Categories of Karma in Vedantic Discussion

Sanchita Karma

Sanchita refers to accumulated karma from past actions that has not yet produced its results.

Prarabdha Karma

Prarabdha refers to that portion of past karma which has begun to produce its effects.

Agami Karma

Agami or Kriyamana refers to actions being performed in the present whose consequences are understood to contribute to future karmic results.

Important Distinction

The doctrine of karma is not identical in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions. For example, Jainism gives karma a distinctive material interpretation, while Buddhism explains karma primarily in relation to intentional action and rejects a permanent self.

07

Sansar: The Cycle of Birth and Death

Sansar refers broadly to the cycle of repeated existence, birth and death. The concept is important in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions, although the philosophical explanation of Sansar differs considerably among them.

Sansar and Karma

In many Indian traditions, karma provides an important explanation for continued existence within Sansar. Actions and their consequences are understood within a moral and causal framework that contributes to continued bondage.

Hindu Philosophical Traditions

Sansar is generally connected with repeated birth and death and with karma. Different schools explain liberation from Sansar through different paths.

Buddhism

Buddhism explains continued existence through dependent origination, craving, ignorance and karma without accepting a permanent self.

Jainism

Jainism explains Sansar in relation to the soul's bondage by karmic matter. Liberation occurs when karmic bondage is removed.

08

Nature of Reality in Indian Philosophy

The nature of reality is one of the central questions of Indian philosophy. Indian philosophical schools did not arrive at one universally accepted answer. Instead, they developed different theories concerning self, matter, consciousness, causation, God, permanence and change.

Major Philosophical Positions

Advaita Vedanta

Brahman is the ultimate non-dual reality. The individual self is ultimately not different from Brahman.

Samkhya

Samkhya distinguishes Purusha, pure consciousness, from Prakriti, the material and productive principle.

Nyaya-Vaisheshika

These traditions defend a pluralistic realism involving substances, qualities, selves and other categories of reality. Nyaya also develops systematic arguments for knowledge and, in later thought, God.

Buddhism

Buddhist traditions emphasize impermanence, dependent origination and the absence of a permanent self. Different Buddhist schools develop different accounts of reality.

Jainism

Jain philosophy recognizes multiple fundamental realities and teaches that reality has many aspects, expressed through the doctrine of Anekantavada.

Charvaka

Charvaka is a materialist school that rejects an eternal soul, afterlife and supernatural explanations, giving primary importance to perception.

Key Point

There is no single Indian theory of reality. The diversity of Indian philosophy is one of its most important intellectual characteristics.

09

Moksha: Liberation

Moksha generally means liberation or release. It is an important soteriological goal in several Indian philosophical traditions, particularly those that accept Sansar and seek freedom from bondage.

The meaning of liberation is not identical across all traditions. In Advaita Vedanta it involves realization of the identity of Atman and Brahman; in other Vedanta schools liberation has different theological and metaphysical interpretations.

Moksha in Different Traditions

Advaita Vedanta

Liberation is associated with knowledge of Brahman and realization that the true self is not different from Brahman.

Samkhya-Yoga

Liberation is connected with discriminative knowledge that distinguishes Purusha from Prakriti. Yoga emphasizes disciplined practice leading to the cessation of mental fluctuations.

Jainism

Liberation occurs when karmic bondage is completely removed and the soul attains its pure state.

Buddhism

Buddhism generally uses the term Nirvana rather than Moksha for the ultimate liberation from suffering, craving and ignorance.

Important Features of Liberation

01

Freedom from bondage and suffering.

02

Connected with knowledge, discipline or spiritual practice depending on the tradition.

03

Often associated with freedom from Sansar.

04

Its exact meaning differs between philosophical schools.

10

Moksha and the Four Purusharthas

Indian philosophical and ethical traditions often discuss four Purusharthas, or aims of human life: Dharma, Artha, Kama and Moksha. These provide a framework for thinking about ethical duty, material well-being, legitimate desires and liberation.

Dharma

Dharma concerns duty, ethical order, right conduct and responsibilities according to the relevant philosophical and social context.

Artha

Artha concerns material prosperity, resources, economic well-being and the means necessary for worldly life.

Kama

Kama concerns desire, enjoyment, affection and aesthetic or sensory fulfillment within an ethical framework.

Moksha

Moksha concerns liberation from bondage and, in traditions accepting Sansar, freedom from the cycle of rebirth.

Key Point

The Purushartha framework presents human life through multiple aims rather than reducing human existence to material achievement alone.

11

Comparative Understanding of Major Concepts

The concepts studied in this unit are interconnected, but they should not be treated as having identical meanings in every philosophical tradition. A comparative approach is necessary for an accurate understanding of Indian philosophy.

Atman

Concerns the nature of the self. Advaita identifies the true self with Brahman, while Buddhism rejects a permanent self and Jainism accepts an enduring conscious Jiva.

Brahman

Concerns ultimate reality, particularly in Upanishadic and Vedanta thought.

Maya

In Advaita, it helps explain the appearance of multiplicity and the relationship between ordinary experience and ultimate reality.

Karma

Concerns action and consequences and is closely related to ethical responsibility and, in many traditions, Sansar.

Moksha

Concerns liberation from bondage. Different traditions define liberation through different philosophical and spiritual frameworks.

12

Conclusion

Indian metaphysical thought contains a rich diversity of theories concerning the self, reality, action and liberation. Atman and Brahman are particularly important in Upanishadic and Vedantic discussions, while Maya has a distinctive role in Advaita Vedanta.

Karma provides an important framework for understanding action and consequences in several Indian traditions. Sansar describes the cycle of repeated existence in traditions that accept it, while liberation is expressed through concepts such as Moksha or, in Buddhism, Nirvana.

The most important academic point is that Indian philosophy is pluralistic. The same term may have different meanings in different traditions, and some traditions reject concepts that are accepted by others. Therefore, these concepts should be studied in their specific philosophical contexts.

Exam Point

Do not write that Atman, Brahman, Maya, Karma and Moksha have exactly the same meaning in every Indian philosophical school. Always mention the relevant philosophical tradition when giving a detailed explanation.
